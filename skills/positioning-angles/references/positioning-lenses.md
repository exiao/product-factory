# Positioning lenses

## Existing behavior and alternatives

Describe an observable behavior in the customer's own terms, without the product. Ask whether the offer can replace a costly or frustrating part of that behavior. Substitution can lower adoption effort, but it still has switching costs and must deliver the promised experience. Do not dismiss stated needs, manufacture guilt, or require addictive mechanics to reproduce a habit.

Compare the actual alternative: another product, manual work, a colleague, inaction, or a different use of attention. A credible frame clarifies when this offer is useful and when the alternative remains better. Borrowed-category comparisons can speed understanding but must not imply capabilities, affiliation, or equivalence that do not exist.

## Consequence, urgency, and substance

The painkiller/vitamin distinction asks how consequential and urgent the need is for this audience. Use evidence of frequency, cost, stakes, and buying triggers. B2B is not automatically urgent and B2C is not automatically optional. Avoid claiming loss framing always wins; pleasure, identity, convenience, and positive outcomes may be the actual value.

Describe the product without decorative adjectives. If it becomes indistinguishable from an alternative, identify a real difference in audience fit, mechanism, workflow, service, price, or distribution. Similarity is a question to investigate, not proof the product has no market.

Ask what changed to make adoption newly feasible or timely when that matters. Verify technology, cultural, regulatory, or economic claims with current evidence. An absent macro shift does not invalidate a stable need. Never manufacture a deadline or demand signal.

## Audience, geography, and personal brands

Adapt framing to observed local language, alternatives, buyer economics, and use context. Do not infer personality, company size, or tone from broad regional stereotypes. Keep the underlying promise truthful across markets.

For a talk, course, newsletter, or event, the alternative may be spending attention elsewhere. State the concrete experience, decision, or takeaway offered; the piece must deliver what the title promises. Do not require all content to imitate a feed or sell urgency.

For a personal brand, find the credible intersection of work, experience, access, and perspective that answers “go to this person when you need ___.” Do not invent biography or expertise. The durable promise should hold across individual pieces even as titles and topics change.

Provocative naming is optional. Evaluate clarity, audience fit, trust, and what the product genuinely supports. Polarization or attention alone does not establish acquisition quality or commercial value.

## Framing families

Use these to generate different hypotheses, not as an exhaustive taxonomy:

| Frame | Decision to test |
| --- | --- |
| Analogy, “X for Y” | Does a familiar reference help this audience understand the offer without misleading them? |
| Category creation | Does a genuinely different experience need a new label, or would an existing category communicate better? |
| Competitive repositioning | Which valued dimension changes how buyers compare their options? |
| Replacement | What specific workaround or incumbent behavior can the product credibly replace? |
| Anti-category | Which frustrating category convention does the product actually avoid, and who cares? |
| Identity | Which authentic behavior or value makes an audience recognize itself in the offer? |
| Outcome | What concrete result can the customer expect, and what evidence supports it? |

Accurate named comparisons can be useful; gratuitous attacks do not establish differentiation. Category creation and novelty are options, not requirements. Test comprehension with the intended audience rather than assuming any follow-up question means failure.

## Express the chosen frame at useful lengths

A one-liner makes the core promise clear; an elevator pitch adds audience and mechanism; a value proposition adds difference and proof; a tagline supports memory; a category label provides orientation. Produce only the forms the user needs. Keep them consistent without imposing fixed word counts or demanding a tagline explain the whole product alone.
