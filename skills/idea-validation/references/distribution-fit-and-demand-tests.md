# Distribution fit and demand tests

Use this reference when an idea's viability depends on reaching buyers, adoption, or payment. Distribution evidence answers a different question from product usefulness, retention, or market size.

## Diagnose readiness and switching

Where adoption or payment is uncertain, distinguish the user, buyer, and approver when they differ. Examine the gap that matters: problem awareness, urgency, active search, current workaround, budget, purchasing ability, trust in this provider, or recurring value. These are diagnostic questions, not mandatory stages or pass/fail gates. Label unknowns and distinguish reported intent from observed action.

Make the benefit concrete: what must the person currently do, decide, remember, or coordinate that this approach removes? Account for any new burden it introduces; fewer clicks alone do not establish less work. Compare that benefit with the effort, risk, setup, trust, and habit change required to adopt it. Include doing nothing or keeping a satisfactory workaround. Design the test around the disputed switching condition; an appealing promise alone does not establish migration or purchase intent. Someone who likes the outcome but cannot approve a purchase needs a different follow-up from someone already satisfied with their solution.

When timing affects the recommendation, explain what changed in technology, behavior, economics, or distribution, what it enables that was previously impractical, and why that matters to this buyer now. A broad trend is context until its connection to the specific offer is supported.

## Compare a primary test channel

Choose the channel that is most plausible for the current test, then keep the choice revisable. Compare:

- **Audience fit:** are likely buyers present in a context where the problem and purchase are relevant?
- **Format fit:** can the value, proof, or search intent be expressed in the channel's native unit?
- **Operator fit:** can the team produce and learn from that unit with its actual time, access, and skills?
- **Reach and cost:** what can be learned with available organic reach or an authorized budget?
- **Measurement fit:** can the path from exposure to qualified action be observed without relying on views alone?

Do not turn these criteria into a permanent one-channel commitment or require the product to be redesigned around a platform before demand is shown.

## Define the repeatable acquisition unit

Describe the smallest unit that could be produced more than once:

1. the audience problem, search intent, or hook that earns attention;
2. the asset or content format and the production effort;
3. the proof or product moment that makes the offer concrete;
4. the next action and destination;
5. the cadence the team can sustain while learning.

The unit should be specific enough to test, yet flexible enough to support several angles. A content idea, a feature, and a scalable acquisition unit are different claims; keep them separate.

## Set a bounded demand test

State the offer, audience, channel, observation window, budget or effort if relevant, measurable outcome, and decision rule. Match the evidence to the real motion:

- interviews can test language and context;
- qualified signups or replies can test interest;
- a pilot, purchase commitment, or paid conversion can test stronger willingness to pay when fulfillment is honest and ready.

Use a baseline or a clearly labeled proposed threshold. High reach with no qualified action may indicate audience, offer, destination, product, or tracking problems; low reach with strong action may indicate a small but qualified audience. Missing downstream data is unknown. Record whether the result supports building, revising the offer or channel, or deferring the idea.
