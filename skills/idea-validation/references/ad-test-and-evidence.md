# Ad test and demand evidence

## Make the idea tangible

Use the real buyer's vocabulary. Name the outcome instead of a technical interchange format or internal architecture. Keep the proposed offer and price visible together. An honest but weak pitch is a reason to inspect the audience, angle, and substitute, not automatically abandon the product.

Separate facts, proposed claims, and unknowns. A fictional or future-feature ad is a concept mockup, not something ready to publish. Never invent adoption, results, customer testimony, scarcity, or a feature to make an idea look stronger.

## Visual comparison

When the user wants to see or feel the ideas, create the appropriate ad-like surface: social card, short-video frame, search result concept, or email mockup. Label concepts and proposed prices clearly. Use natural card heights with sensible minimums and responsive media, not a fixed-height shell that clips the offer on mobile. Inspect a narrow viewport and ensure every pitch and price is visible.

Use actual supplied assets or available authorized image generation when useful. A photo is not mandatory if the argument is better shown with a simple diagram or truthful product mock. Keep the gallery local unless publishing is requested; no assumed Surge project or fixed host.

## Test the risky assumption

Identify a reachable buyer category and a concrete question: is the problem frequent, costly, urgent, funded, or underserved? Distinguish what a person says from what they do. Interviews reveal language and context; qualified signups reveal interest; a pilot or purchase commitment can provide stronger willingness-to-pay evidence, subject to honest terms and fulfillment capability.

Propose a bounded test matched to the product's real sales motion. State the offer, target audience, channel, observation period, budget if spending is in scope, success/failure evidence, and what decision follows. Avoid universal conversion thresholds or treating a handful of friends as a market sample.

The Ad Test is a framing exercise. Market size needs a defensible buyer count, spend assumptions, accessible share, and uncertainty. Do not label an intuitive pitch score as TAM. Distribution evidence, sustainable unit economics, and repeat use answer different questions; collect the evidence that threatens the current decision first.
