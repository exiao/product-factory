# Product workflow

Read for delegated product work that needs discovery or direction decisions. Apply the routing, evidence and handoff rules in ../SKILL.md. Follow the fixed stage order in ../SKILL.md; the numbered details below expand those stages. Reuse settled decisions and sufficient artifacts under that entrypoint’s completion rules. Complete the requested outcome, including downstream authorized work.

## Research and agree on the people problem

1. **Provisional vision:** use [$product-vision](../../product-vision/SKILL.md) to capture the user's intended person, situation, outcome, priorities and boundaries. Separate explicit user choices from interpretations of supplied documents. A portfolio recommendation, competitor positioning or prior prototype is evidence to examine, not permission to substitute a different audience or job. Surface conflicting interpretations early. Before dependent problem or solution decisions, use [$domain-modeling](../../domain-modeling/SKILL.md) for ambiguous concepts and the optional `explore-unknowns` skill for consequential unknowns when available; otherwise identify the known facts, open questions, assumptions and blind spots directly. Keep definitions and open questions in the existing record; reuse settled meanings. This is part of early framing, not two mandatory standalone exercises.
2. **Desk research:** for unresolved product discovery, research the domain on the web before committing to the problem model or solution. Investigate how people actually do the work, its component tasks and dependencies, varied starting situations, existing workarounds, and current alternatives. Prefer practitioner accounts, primary research, domain teaching and official product documentation; search snippets alone are not evidence. Keep a compact linked evidence ledger distinguishing reported practice, expert guidance, vendor claims and inference. Desk research does not establish prevalence, demand or product effectiveness. Reuse sufficient recent research, explaining what it covers and what needs refreshing; respect an explicit no-browsing constraint and disclose evidence gaps. Stop when the material framing questions are supported or clearly identified as requiring user/customer input, rather than collecting a fixed source quota.
3. **Problem breakdown and empathy map:** read and use [$people-problems](../../people-problems/SKILL.md) to articulate a curated list of human, solution-agnostic problems with evidence and a solved state before naming features. Read and use [$empathy-maps](../../empathy-maps/SKILL.md) to visualize each relevant user type in a defined task context. Use the map to expose needs, tensions and evidence gaps. Populate it from relevant desk research and published first-person accounts; lack of interviews conducted by us is not a reason to leave it empty. Where useful, explore remaining questions with clearly labeled synthetic interviews through that skill. Preserve speaker attribution and distinguish reported evidence, interpretation and simulation.
4. **Problem checkpoint:** show the concise problem statements and relevant supporting artifact, explain what research changed, and present the three answers from people-problems for each priority problem: what the problem is, how we know it is real, and how we will know it is solved. Resolve material ambiguity about audience, job, pain or finish line with the user before solution-dependent work. Ask a focused question about the disputed interpretation, not a generic “looks good?” Reuse explicit agreement already in the conversation; if the user delegated the choice, state the chosen framing and its assumptions and proceed. While a required answer is pending, continue only independent research or other work that cannot preempt it.
5. **Outcome criteria:** use [$acceptance-criteria](../../acceptance-criteria/SKILL.md) to turn each priority problem's success definition into observable criteria and an evidence plan before selecting the solution. Explain why each measure indicates progress on the person's problem; business metrics may support this account but do not replace it. Preserve exclusions and unresolved contracts. Treat a proposed prototype milestone separately from the user's full promised outcome; do not silently reduce the latter to something easier to demonstrate.

Carry these statements and their evidence through idea selection, storyboards, prototypes and verification. Revisit them when evidence changes; do not rewrite the problem merely to fit the chosen feature.

## Compare mechanisms, then narrow the prototype

After problem framing and before selecting approaches, read and use [$strategy-kernel](../../strategy-kernel/SKILL.md) when direction depends on a strategic obstacle, resource constraints, or a consequential choice about where to focus. Capture the diagnosis, guiding policy, source of power, and coherent actions in the shared project record; use them to compare approaches and make exclusions explicit. Reuse the vision and problem evidence, label unsupported diagnoses as hypotheses, and preserve settled choices. Skip a separate strategy exercise when the direction is already clear.

6. **Alternative approaches:** for an unresolved solution direction, explore meaningfully different ways to address the agreed priority problem before designing screens. Use [$quick-brainstorm](../../quick-brainstorm/SKILL.md) when useful. Alternatives should differ in mechanism or workflow, not merely names, visual styles or customer segments. For each, explain the problem addressed, how it could help, existing alternatives, tradeoffs, hardest assumption and smallest useful test. Include a simpler workflow or non-product intervention when plausible. Explain what the person currently has to do, decide, remember, or coordinate that each approach would remove, and any new burden it creates; fewer clicks alone do not establish less work. Recommend a direction with reasons; any estimated probabilities are subjective judgments, not measured demand. Do not manufacture alternatives to reopen a direction the user has already selected.

Before storyboarding the shortlist, pressure-test each mechanism on the same concrete starting material and desired outcome. Show the causal chain: what the person supplies, what the intervention actually does, what changes in the result, and why that change could outperform their current workaround. Name the central tension it resolves, the work it transfers back to the person, and a plausible reason to reject it. Feature categories, generic advice, and a button promising a good result do not establish a mechanism. Keep this comparison compact in the existing project record; no separate ceremony is needed.

When usefulness depends on output quality, create a worked sample for the leading mechanisms before investing in dependent UI. Use the appropriate medium: audible musical alternatives, an edited passage, a computed answer, or a manually delivered service result. Compare against the same input and current workaround using the agreed outcome criteria, including speed and user control where relevant. A curated or manually prepared sample tests the potential value of the result, not reliable automation; disclose how it was made. If execution is unavailable, show a concrete worked scenario and mark output quality untested. Do not call that direction validated or replace the missing demonstration with more screens. Reuse existing samples and proceed within delegated choices; preserve an explicit request for a UI-only deliverable while stating what it cannot test.

7. **Illustrated storyboards:** read and use [$storyboards](../../storyboards/SKILL.md) to illustrate the consequential alternatives. Ground the person and need in the agreed problem and available contextual evidence (including an empathy map when used), then show a concrete scenario unfolding over time through images and short captions: context, actions, relevant interactions and consequences. A current-state story can expose a workaround and its breakdown; a proposed story can show how an alternative might change the outcome. Compare alternatives using the same actor, scenario and goal; use separate stories for different jobs. Preserve evidence and uncertainty labels; a proposed outcome is a hypothesis, not proof. The storyboard communicates a lived scenario rather than repeating the empathy quadrants or becoming a screen flow. Keep fidelity low enough to invite changes. Produce and inspect the actual illustrated artifact; captions or a prompt alone do not complete an illustrated request unless the user explicitly requests text-only output.
8. **Direction checkpoint:** present the concrete alternatives, recommendation, illustrated storyboards, applicable worked samples and their limits, and what each prototype would test. Obtain the user's selection of the direction or comparison set before building dependent UI prototypes or implementation, unless that selection is already explicit or the user delegated it. A request to “build prototypes” authorizes prototyping work but does not settle an ambiguous audience or choose an unreviewed solution. Confirmation can select several ideas for comparison; it need not pick a single winner. Silence and elapsed time are not confirmation.

9. **Interaction design and clickable low-fi flow:** after the direction checkpoint, read and use [$interaction-design](../../interaction-design/SKILL.md) to translate the accepted scenario or storyboard and outcome criteria into interaction decisions, a wireflow, and a clickable low-fidelity prototype before high-fidelity visual work or polish. Interaction-design owns behavior and the handoff brief; it uses [$design-mode](../../design-mode/SKILL.md) for prototype rendering and verification. Reuse a settled prior low-fi artifact and change only the unresolved portions. Exercise coherent real states and the relevant simulated limits (for example, fixed data, unavailable integrations, or a missing backend), and label what is simulated. Skip this pass for a clear small fix or an already approved high-fidelity direction; it is not an extra approval gate.

Before building the first UI for an accepted scenario, select one core loop per prototype direction and identify features and steps to omit. Keep these exclusions in the existing brief. Compare alternative mechanisms on the same task; do not combine their capabilities to make the prototype feel complete. Then make the **idea-to-interaction bridge** visible. Reuse the mechanism and worked sample from step 6: show what the person brings, what the product contributes, what the person changes or decides, and the resulting state or next iteration. A human activity loop such as “write, try, revise” does not specify the product’s contribution. Storyboard approval settles the scenario, not an unseen interface.

Use step 9 to turn that bridge into a compact wireflow with actual triggering controls, the content or assistance revealed, and keep/undo/back behavior. Show it alongside the first clickable low-fi artifact; reuse an existing wireflow when sufficient. This is a handoff requirement, not another approval ceremony. Preserve explicit requests for low-fi and follow design-mode’s rendering contract, including actual Wired Elements where required; polished styling or plain grayscale controls are not a substitute.

Start the low-fi surface with the current object and immediate task. Do not convert every storyboard beat, craft principle, or supporting task into a feature. Omit help, history, notes, and secondary views unless the selected loop or explicit requirements need them. For capabilities that remain, prefer contextual assistance on the current object and keep essential actions discoverable. Remove preview or setup steps that add no meaningful choice while preserving necessary consent and control. Inspect the initial screen without narration and exercise the decisive transition before adding visual polish. Verify both that the action is understandable and that the product’s contribution is concrete; a functional click path alone passes neither usefulness nor visual hierarchy.

During alternative exploration, identify the uncertainty most likely to change the direction: user value, adoption, usability, technical feasibility, delivery cost, or operational fit. Choose the smallest experiment that can resolve it. A technical spike, output-quality sample, manual-service trial, or buyer conversation may be more informative than another screen. Keep experiments within existing authorization; they can inform alternatives without committing to dependent UI implementation or bypassing unresolved user decisions. Use the acceptance criteria to distinguish learning, user outcomes, and release readiness.

When deciding whether to invest in a commercial product or paid offer, read and use [$idea-validation](../../idea-validation/SKILL.md) to examine buyer readiness, reach, willingness-to-pay evidence, and delivery economics before substantial implementation. Reuse the shared brief and evidence. Internal tools and already-approved delivery tasks do not need a new commercial-validation exercise.

During concept comparison or at the first usable prototype, read and use [$desirability-study](../../desirability-study/SKILL.md) when the unresolved decision concerns perceived qualities, personal relevance, or conditional willingness to adopt. Inspect the actual stimuli and keep appearance, relevance, and adoption findings separate; broader adoption questions need a neutral proposition covering benefits and commitments. Use supplied participant evidence, prepare a study when responses are unavailable, or use isolated synthetic participants only when simulation is requested or established in context. Synthetic reactions remain hypotheses, not evidence of customer demand. This pass supplements the prototype UX walkthrough; successful task completion does not establish desire to use. Reuse the shared brief and evidence with idea-validation, which owns the commercial build decision and demand-test plan. Do not add a mandatory study or duplicate decision checkpoint when these questions are already settled.

Empathy maps, illustrated storyboards and the first-prototype walkthrough are required in the default product workflow. Keep them concise and reuse sufficient existing artifacts. Worked output comparisons supplement storyboards; they do not replace them. Follow the entrypoint's rules for explicit scope changes and unavailable artifacts. No hosted approval page or separate ceremony is required.

Journey mapping is optional: add it when the decision depends on sequence, touchpoints, handoffs, or friction and recovery across stages, or when the user requests it. Read and use [$journey-mapping](../../journey-mapping/SKILL.md) in those cases; choose a journey map for a specific product/service scenario or an experience map for broader product-agnostic activity. Label unobserved paths as assumptions. Do not require an extra map merely to complete a standard set of deliverables.

## Check in at decision boundaries

Keep the user involved while useful work continues. Check in once at each meaningful boundary: research/problem framing, alternatives/direction selection, and the first usable prototype before expanding or polishing it substantially. Combine adjacent checkpoints when the same exchange can resolve them. Show what changed, the relevant artifact, the remaining uncertainty and the next decision. Check in immediately if new evidence changes the audience, job, priority, scope or recommendation.

Lead each checkpoint with the current problem or decision, relevant visual artifact, and recommended next step. Put detailed research, test logs, and superseded explorations in linked supporting material or an appendix. Clearly identify the current direction so the user need not sort through old prototypes to review the work.

Distinguish a progress update from a decision request. Updates do not require a response. Required unresolved decisions pause dependent work; settled or delegated decisions do not need repeated approval. During longer work, give concise progress updates at least every 60 seconds when tool execution permits, focused on findings and implications rather than activity. Respect the user's requested cadence and autonomy. At the first prototype, let the user try the decisive interaction and expose any gap between the simulated behavior and the promised outcome; continue already-authorized work while awaiting optional feedback. Recheck the earlier exclusions and remove new burdens that do not improve that outcome; do not wait until this checkpoint to consider subtraction. For an existing product, check whether the addition burdens other users through distraction, extra steps, relearning, or loss of a familiar path; verify the affected existing task where practical.

Launch the prototype at its own working URL in a separate tab or window, with the product interface independent of artifact-review. Keep review approval controls outside that experience. Verify the URL and decisive interaction before handoff; use a server that survives the agent turn when local review continues afterward.

Before testing or handing off a prototype, state its audience, task, starting state, intended stopping point, and what is scripted versus functional. Specify whether variants are independent alternatives or connected steps. A feature outside the prototype's declared scope is a limitation or future product expectation, not automatically a defect; the full promised outcome still needs its own evidence.

## Synthetic UX pass and revision

On the first usable prototype, check the decisive interaction and relevant recovery before substantial expansion or polish. Use [$synthetic-userstudies](../../synthetic-userstudies/SKILL.md) for the required synthetic UX walkthrough. Reuse a sufficient prior walkthrough of the current prototype; real-user observations can supplement it. Follow an explicit user request for a different evaluation method, recording its coverage and limits. A technical execution check alone does not establish human comprehension. Choose a walkthrough or variant comparison for the agreed audience and task; use adversarial mode only when requested. An earlier concept or storyboard pass can inform ideation but does not establish prototype behavior or usability. Assessment-only work stops at its requested scope.

When using synthetic-userstudies, follow that skill's screenshot-first workflow for visual products: inspect the initial screen without guided actions, then exercise the decisive interaction and relevant recovery states. Deliver actual annotated screenshots with SYNTHETIC persona reactions, separate from observed interface behavior. Pass the prototype boundaries and original outcome criteria to delegated reviewers, and check their actual coverage before reporting completion. A functional PASS or favorable persona reaction does not establish clear visual hierarchy, customer demand, or product effectiveness.

Show a short synthesis at the first-prototype checkpoint: what worked, reproduced defects, provisional friction hypotheses, and proposed changes. Fix verified defects within scope, make justified reversible refinements within the selected direction, and recheck the affected interactions and screenshots. Ask only when a proposed change reopens a material product decision. Preserve unresolved hypotheses and the next real-user validation; do not iterate until personas merely praise the design. Disclose missing capture or execution access and the resulting coverage gap rather than claiming the pass is complete.

Do not mandate a specific brainstorming tool, hosted gate page, or permanent specialist profiles.

## Design and refine criteria

10. **Design and prototypes:** carry the interaction brief and prototype from step 9 into [$design-mode](../../design-mode/SKILL.md) and [$impeccable](../../impeccable/SKILL.md) for high-fidelity design. If interaction work is already settled, reuse it directly; if a material behavioral gap remains, use [$interaction-design](../../interaction-design/SKILL.md) only for that gap. Do not repeat discovery, alternative selection, or prototype work already completed. Keep choices traceable to the vision, agreed problems, empathy maps, storyboards, outcome criteria, and any optional journey or experience maps. Provide working prototypes when interaction is the disputed issue or the user requests them. Prototype checks should cover actual interactions and label simulation.
11. **Detailed acceptance criteria:** use [$acceptance-criteria](../../acceptance-criteria/SKILL.md) again as the direction becomes specific, before implementing each slice. Add observable interaction behavior and relevant empty, invalid, permission, failure, and recovery cases with an evidence plan. Retain the original outcome criteria; detailed checks supplement them rather than replacing or weakening the promised improvement.

## Build and prove

Use [$program-design](../../program-design/SKILL.md) when code structure needs a decision. Pass the original criteria, source artifacts, exclusions, and evidence plan directly to any delegated work. Delegate bounded independent tasks when useful; own integration and follow-through here. Do not create user-owned tasks, boards, or recurring automations unless requested.

Implement coherent slices, review using [$code-review](../../code-review/SKILL.md), and verify using [$verify-feature](../../verify-feature/SKILL.md) with evidence matched to each criterion. Existing tests and CI count for what they demonstrate; deployed behavior needs deployed evidence only when that is the promised result. Do not require production deployment to validate a local code change.

Fix observed defects within the authorized scope and rerun affected checks. Return to the affected decision and artifacts when evidence invalidates the framing or the user corrects it. A correction to an unconfirmed assumption is not a change to an approved agreement. Distinguish that from a new preference after an explicit decision.

If deployment is requested, use the appropriate deployment workflow and [$verify-deploy](../../verify-deploy/SKILL.md). Report actual completion, criterion status, links or artifact paths, and remaining limitations. Stop when the agreed finish line is met.
