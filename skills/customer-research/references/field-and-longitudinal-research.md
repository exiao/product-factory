# Field observation and longitudinal research

Read for requests to prepare, conduct or analyze contextual interviews or diary studies. Reuse the decision and available evidence; do not make fieldwork a prerequisite for every synthesis.

## Choose the study

Reuse the specific answer needed, who can answer it, the decision it changes and the available time. Match the method: contextual observation for actual work and surroundings; interviews for accounts and meanings; diaries for change over time; usability sessions for task performance; card sorting for candidate groupings and tree testing for an existing hierarchy. Surveys can quantify clearly defined responses in a described sample; survey reports of behavior are still self-reports.

When preparing fieldwork, connect eligibility and recruitment, guide, stimulus and pilot, collection, and synthesis. Scale the plan to the request rather than imposing fixed lead times or participant counts. Use the discussion guide as a flexible outline: preserve coverage of the research question while following relevant episodes and surprises. Log material protocol deviations. A numerical summary of a few qualitative sessions remains a sample description, not a representative estimate.

## Context and actual work

Study the setting where the relevant activity occurs, physically or remotely where access permits. Capture the goal, trigger, environment, people, artifacts, constraints and current workaround. Let the participant show a recent or typical task using their tools where appropriate; ask neutral follow-ups after observing the consequential action rather than teaching a route. Preserve what was said, demonstrated and inferred separately. A staged demonstration may differ from natural work.

Ask about important infrequent tasks as well as frequent ones, and about work outside the product. If the participant reconstructs an episode, retain its sequence and ask what is missing. When observation conflicts with an account, inspect context and alternative explanations rather than diagnosing the person or silently replacing one source.

## Diary protocol

Use diaries when waiting, repeated activity or changes over time are central. Define an eligible situation, observation period and manageable entry trigger. Prefer entries near the event; use periodic check-ins where silence or waiting is itself relevant. Capture event time, action, expectation, result and available screenshots or other artifacts. Distinguish a missing entry from no event or no difficulty.

Pilot instructions and recording burden. Preserve protocol changes, recruitment filters, researcher prompts, assigned activities, incentives and exclusions. Record started/completed participation, attrition and missing periods. Assigned behavior and rewards can change the experience; do not treat study compliance as spontaneous demand.

Select follow-up interviews for informative differences, contradictions and unresolved episodes as well as engaged participants. Probe with the participant's entries and artifacts. State any bias from following up only with completers. Keep per-person timelines before cross-person synthesis.

## Method and decision record

Retain the decision, audience/recruitment, task wording, stimulus version, exposure/order, assistance, outcome definition and evidence locations when relevant. Keep planned, conducted and analyzed samples separate, with unusable sessions and reasons visible. A short study can use a short record.

For concept reactions, capture current use of alternatives, what already works and the incremental change the concept offers. Separate comprehension, appeal, task performance and real commitment. Positive ratings of an already available capability do not establish unmet demand.
