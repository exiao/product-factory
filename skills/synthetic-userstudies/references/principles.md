# Research reasoning for synthetic sessions

## Behavior, attitudes, and evidence

Use specific episodes to explore circumstances, actions, alternatives, constraints, and consequences. Ask what happened before asking for an explanation. Retrospective accounts can contain rationalization; stated preferences reveal attitudes and expectations, not necessarily behavior. Both can be useful when labeled correctly. In a synthetic session, neither is a real customer observation.

Probe neutrally: "What happened next?", "What made that difficult?", or "What were you weighing?" Follow the useful thread rather than requiring a fixed number of "whys." Ask one question at a time. Avoid presupposing frustration, urgency, or dissatisfaction. Explore occasions when the current solution worked and when the proposed problem did not matter.

## Interview progression

Use a flexible progression: situation and goal → recent episode → current behavior and alternatives → consequences and tradeoffs → exceptions and open questions. Do not pitch a solution during problem discovery. A participant can be indifferent, unable to recall, satisfied with the status quo, or constrained by another priority.

Compare accounts for recurring hypotheses and contradictions. Two similar accounts do not establish prevalence or saturation, and two synthetic personas are not two independent customer observations. Do not force agreement or discard a consequential minority/accessibility concern because it lacks votes.

## Research phases

| Phase | Question | Appropriate use of simulation | Real-world follow-up |
|---|---|---|---|
| Right Problems | What need and circumstances deserve investigation? | Rehearse interviews and generate competing problem hypotheses | Contextual observation, real interviews, appropriate quantitative research |
| Right Solutions | How might a concept address the need? | Explore interpretations, alternatives, and possible objections | Concept/prototype research with the intended audience |
| Right Way | Where might the interaction fail? | Anticipate friction and guide inspection | Observed usability sessions, accessibility checks, runtime verification, experiments where appropriate |

Problem discovery does not require a Promise or Product. Introducing a concept changes the question; explicitly switch phase and distinguish hypothetical reactions from observed choices. A live agent interaction tests the product state, not a human's comprehension or preference.

## Synthesis

Keep supplied customer evidence, observed product behavior, simulated reactions, and analyst inferences separate. Cite source locations for factual claims. Synthetic quotes illustrate generated scenarios; they never prove customer patterns.

State the decision, hypothesis, supporting evidence and limits, plausible competing explanation, and the next validation. Describe what result would change the recommendation. Match the validation to the claim: reproduction for a broken control, human usability testing for comprehension, and appropriately designed research or experiments for preference, demand, and causal effects.

Methodological background: [NN/g on synthetic users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/synthetic-users/). Its general findings do not validate any particular simulated persona or product recommendation.
